[CausVid: From Slow Bidirectional to Fast Autoregressive Video Diffusion Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07772)

CausVid, by Tianwei Yin, Qiang Zhang, and collaborators, extends ideas from DMD to video. What I find especially interesting is that the method changes both the sampling speed and the attention structure: it distills a many-step **bidirectional** video diffusion teacher into a few-step **causal** student.

The paper reports an initial latency of 1.3 seconds, after which the model generates video continuously at approximately 9.4 frames per second on an H100 GPU. Compared with its bidirectional teacher, which has an initial latency of 219.2 seconds and a throughput of 0.6 frames per second, CausVid reduces initial latency by approximately 169× and increases throughput by approximately 16×. 

But autoregressive generation has its own challenge: an error in an early chunk can affect the chunks generated after it. CausVid addresses the problem through a combination of **asymmetric distillation** and **ODE initialization**. I find this paper an important step toward later work on autoregressive diffusion distillation, including the forcing-based methods that motivated my reading.

## Why Generate Video Autoregressively?

A bidirectional video diffusion model can use information from both earlier and later frames while denoising. This helps it model temporal consistency, but the model generally needs to process the entire clip before it can deliver the result. A causal model instead generates the video in order, so it can begin returning output before the full video is finished.

CausVid applies causality at the **chunk level**. A chunk can attend to earlier chunks and to frames within the same chunk, but not to future chunks. This is more precise than saying that every frame can see only earlier frames: attention is bidirectional *within* a chunk and causal *between* chunks.

The model works in a compressed video latent space. In the paper's setup, a chunk of five latent frames represents 16 video frames. The student is initialized from the pretrained video diffusion transformer, with its attention changed to enforce this chunk-wise structure. [CausVid, Section 4.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.07772v4#S4.SS1)

## Bidirectional Teacher to Causal Student

A natural approach would be to first convert the bidirectional teacher into a causal diffusion model and then distill that causal teacher into a faster student:

$$
\text{bidirectional model}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{causal teacher}
\;\longrightarrow\;\text{few-step causal student}.
$$

However, the authors find that this approach performs less well in their experiments. The causal teacher is already weaker than the bidirectional model, and errors that accumulate during its generation can also affect the student. This does not mean that a causal teacher is always a bad choice; it describes the comparison made in CausVid.

The paper instead uses **asymmetric distillation**:

$$
\text{bidirectional teacher}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{causal student}.
$$

The asymmetry is in what the two models can attend to. During training, the teacher can use information across the video when estimating its score, while the student remains restricted to its current and previous chunks. The teacher therefore supplies a richer training signal without changing the student's causal attention at inference. The student does not need to see future chunks when it is generating a video. [CausVid, Section 4.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.07772v4#S4.SS2)

## Applying DMD to Video

The DMD idea is the same as in the previous posts, but the samples are now videos rather than individual images. CausVid uses:

- A frozen **bidirectional teacher** to estimate the target video score.
- A **fake-score model** trained to estimate the distribution of the student's current outputs.
- A **causal student** updated using the difference between these two score estimates.

Intuitively, the teacher's score tells the student how to move its generated video toward the target distribution, while the fake score accounts for what the student is already producing. As in DMD, these are estimates of scores on noisy samples; the practical update is an approximate distribution-matching gradient.

The paper's training algorithm samples video chunks at different noise levels, obtains clean predictions from the causal student, and then adds noise to those predictions for the score comparison. The fake-score model is trained on the student's generated outputs and updated more frequently than the student, following the two-time-scale idea from DMD2. At inference, only the causal student is needed. [CausVid, Sections 3.2 and 4.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.07772v4#S4.SS2)

The result is a four-step student distilled from a 50-step teacher. The two kinds of steps should not be confused: the student generates **chunks sequentially**, and it uses a small number of **denoising steps within each chunk**.

## ODE Initialization

Directly applying the DMD update to the causal student can be unstable because its attention differs from the bidirectional teacher's. CausVid first gives the student a useful starting point with **ODE initialization**.

The procedure is:

1. Sample a noise sequence and run the bidirectional teacher with an ODE solver to produce a video denoising trajectory.
2. Save intermediate noisy states at timesteps used by the student, together with the final clean teacher-generated video.
3. Train the causal student to predict the clean video from the selected noisy states using a regression loss.
4. Use the resulting model as the initialization for asymmetric DMD training.

For noisy latent chunks $$\{x_{t^i}^i\}$$ and their teacher-generated clean targets $$\{x_0^i\}$$, the initialization objective is

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{init}}
=\mathbb E\left[
\left\|
G_\phi\bigl(\{x_{t^i}^i\},\{t^i\}\bigr)
-\{x_0^i\}
\right\|_2^2
\right].
$$

Here, $$G_\phi$$ is the causal student, and $$t^i$$ is the noise timestep for chunk $$i$$. The target is the **teacher-generated clean output**, not necessarily a real training video. The regression gives the student an initial denoising ability before distribution matching begins; it does not by itself eliminate error accumulation. In the reported setup, the authors generate 1,000 ODE pairs for this initialization. [CausVid, Sections 4.3 and 5](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.07772v4#S4.SS3)

This also helps me distinguish CausVid from DMD2: DMD2 removes the paired regression loss from its main training objective, whereas CausVid uses teacher-generated ODE pairs for a separate initialization stage before DMD training.

## Inference: Generating a Video Chunk by Chunk

At inference, the student starts with noise for the first chunk and denoises it in a few steps. Once that chunk is generated, the model uses it as context while generating the next chunk. It continues in this way, producing video as it goes.

CausVid uses **KV caching** to reuse the attention keys and values from previously generated chunks, reducing repeated computation. The model can also use a sliding window to continue beyond the clip length used during training. This makes long generation possible, although it does not guarantee perfect consistency indefinitely. [CausVid, Section 4.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.07772v4#S4.SS4)

In the paper's experiments, the causal student substantially reduces latency while maintaining quality competitive with the bidirectional teacher. The authors also report trade-offs: compared with the teacher, the student performs worse in temporal flickering and output diversity in their ablation. [CausVid, Section 5.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.07772v4#S5.SS2)

For me, the central idea is that a powerful bidirectional model can supervise a student with a different, causal architecture. ODE initialization makes that transition easier to train, and DMD then helps turn the causal model into a fast video generator.

## Python-Style Pseudocode

The following sketch brings the three stages together. All videos are represented as latent chunks. `student` predicts clean latents with chunk-wise causal attention; `teacher` and `fake_score` use bidirectional attention. Text conditioning and optimizer details are omitted for readability. This is explanatory pseudocode adapted from [CausVid, Algorithms 1–2 and Section 4.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.07772v4#S4).

```python
# Helpers:
# add_noise(x0, t, eps) = alpha(t) * x0 + sigma(t) * eps
# optimize(model, loss) clears gradients, backpropagates, and steps its optimizer.
# stop_gradient(x) detaches x from the computation graph.
# Timesteps descend from full noise to clean; four student evaluations per chunk.
schedule = [T, t3, t2, t1, 0]

teacher = freeze(pretrained_bidirectional_model)
student = copy_with_chunk_causal_attention(teacher)
fake_score = trainable_copy(teacher)


# Stage 1: ODE initialization with teacher-generated trajectories.
ode_pairs = []
for _ in range(num_ode_pairs):
    with no_grad():
        trajectory = solve_teacher_ode(teacher, randn(video_shape), schedule)
    ode_pairs.append(trajectory)

for _ in range(init_iterations):
    trajectory = sample(ode_pairs)
    times = sample_per_chunk(schedule)
    noisy_chunks = stack([trajectory[t][i] for i, t in enumerate(times)])
    clean_target = trajectory[0]
    prediction = student(noisy_chunks, times)
    optimize(student, mse(prediction, clean_target))


# Stage 2: asymmetric distribution matching.
for step in range(dmd_iterations):
    real_chunks = sample_latent_video(training_data)
    times = sample_per_chunk(schedule)
    inputs = add_noise(real_chunks, times, randn_like(real_chunks))
    generated = student(inputs, times)  # One causally masked forward pass.
    detached_generated = stop_gradient(generated)

    # Five fake-score updates per student update in the reported setup.
    t = sample_diffusion_time()  # One shared time for the whole video.
    if step % 5 == 0:
        noisy_generated = add_noise(generated, t, randn_like(generated))
        with no_grad():
            real_score = teacher.score(noisy_generated, t)
            current_score = fake_score.score(noisy_generated, t)
            direction = dmd_weight(t) * (current_score - real_score)

        # Surrogate loss: gradients flow only through the student output.
        loss_dmd = mean(stop_gradient(direction) * generated)
        optimize(student, loss_dmd)

    eps = randn_like(detached_generated)
    noisy_generated = add_noise(detached_generated, t, eps)
    predicted_eps = fake_score.predict_noise(noisy_generated, t)
    optimize(fake_score, mse(predicted_eps, eps))


# Stage 3: streaming inference; only the trained student is needed.
@no_grad()
def generate_video(student, num_chunks):
    cache = empty_kv_cache()
    for _ in range(num_chunks):
        x = randn(chunk_shape)
        for t, next_t in zip(schedule[:-1], schedule[1:]):
            clean_prediction = student(x, t, cache=cache)
            x = add_noise(clean_prediction, next_t, randn_like(x))

        # alpha(0) = 1 and sigma(0) = 0, so x is now a clean chunk.
        # Cache a separate forward pass of that clean chunk at time zero.
        new_kv = student.extract_kv(x, t=0, cache=cache)
        cache.append(new_kv)
        yield vae_decode(x)
```

Here, `dmd_weight(t)` abstracts the timestep weighting and normalization used in the practical DMD gradient. The surrogate loss shows its direction without differentiating through either score estimate. During distillation, the student receives noisy **real video chunks** at independently sampled timesteps; the sequential chunk generation loop is used at inference.
